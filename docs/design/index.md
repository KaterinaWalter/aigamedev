## 🔗 How the Frameworks Connect

The frameworks come from different authors, so they use different words for related ideas. Here's how they line up:

| Idea | Where it shows up |
|:--|:--|
| **What the player does** | MDA calls these *mechanics*. LM-GM's list of *game mechanics* is broader, and includes some things MDA would call dynamics (like Feedback or Behavioral Momentum). |
| **What the player feels** | MDA's *aesthetics*, described with LeBlanc's 8 kinds of fun |
| **What the player learns** | LM-GM's *learning mechanics*, ordered by Bloom's thinking skills |
| **Feedback** | A game mechanic in LM-GM, and the focus of Gee's Interaction, Risk Taking, and Just in Time principles |
| **Scaffolding** | LM-GM mechanics like Tutorial, Guidance, and Repetition, and the focus of Gee's Well-Ordered Problems and Challenge and Consolidation principles |
| **Context vs. content** | Squire's core argument, echoed by Gee: learning a field means learning to "play its game," not memorizing its facts |

## 🎮 Why Gee?

Two pages in this section draw on James Paul Gee. His argument is simple: <span class="highlighter">good video games already use good learning principles.</span> If nobody could learn a game, nobody would buy it, and players won't accept games that are easy, dumbed down, or short. So commercial games have had to get very good at teaching hard, long, complex things in a way people enjoy.

He also pushes back on the idea that "all you learn from a game is how to play the game." A field like biology isn't just a set of facts. It's a "game" that biologists play, with its own activities, tools, language, and rules. Learning biology *should* mean learning to play that game. This lines up closely with Squire's argument on the [Aligning Objectives]({% link docs/design/aligning-objectives.md %}) page.

Gee lists 16 principles in total. This site covers nine of them, split across [Feedback Design]({% link docs/design/feedback-design.md %}) and [Scaffolding & Difficulty]({% link docs/design/scaffolding-difficulty.md %}).

## ⚖️ Skill Goals vs. Attitude Goals

Before you start, figure out which kind of goal your game has, because the advice in this section depends on it.

<html>
  <div>
<dl>
  <dt>Teaching a skill or concept</dt>
  <dd>The learning should be the core of play. If you could remove the learning content and the game would play the same, it's bolted on. Use the first four pages.</dd>
  <dt>Shifting an attitude or behavior</dt>
  <dd>Being direct tends to backfire, so some off-message content and indirect framing are intentional. Use the Attitude & Behavior Change page, alongside the others.</dd>
</dl>
  </div>
</html>

<div class="imp" markdown="block">

Many games have both kinds of goals. A public health game might teach how disease spreads (a concept) *and* aim to build support for vaccination (an attitude).

> **NOTE:** When that happens, apply the skill advice to the parts of the game that teach the concept, and the attitude advice to how the message is framed and delivered.
